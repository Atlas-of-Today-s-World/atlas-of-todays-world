The extraction economy of the Andes is older than any state in it. Silver from
Potosi financed an empire on another continent, and the labour that produced it
was organised by conscription. That sequence, a resource of global value with a
distant beneficiary and a local population bearing the cost, set a template that
independence did not dissolve.

## Why the pattern persisted

Colonial extraction built infrastructure pointing outward: roads to ports rather
than between provinces. It concentrated administrative capacity where the
resource was, and it established a revenue habit in which the central state
lived off a royalty rather than off broad taxation. Both features survived into
the republics.

The consequence shows up as a specific political vulnerability. A government
financed by mineral rent does not need a functioning income tax, and therefore
never has to strike the bargain with citizens that building one requires. When
prices are high the state can spend without asking; when they fall it must
suddenly ask for a great deal.

## The current cycle

Copper and lithium have made the central Andes strategically important again,
for reasons entirely unlike the last boom: electrification rather than coinage.
Chile and Peru together account for a very large share of world copper output,
and the salt flats spanning Chile, Bolivia and Argentina hold a substantial part
of known lithium reserves.

Three approaches are being tried at once. Chile has moved toward state
participation in lithium through public-private arrangements. Bolivia has kept
the resource in state hands and struggled to industrialise it. Argentina's
provinces have negotiated separately and attracted faster private investment.
None of the three has yet shown that it can convert the resource into a
diversified economy, which is the goal every one of them states.

## What has genuinely changed

Two things. First, water. Lithium extraction in the salt flats draws on brine in
some of the driest inhabited places on earth, and the communities affected are
now parties to the negotiation rather than subjects of it; consultation
requirements carry real legal force in several jurisdictions.

Second, the buyers. Demand sits in battery supply chains whose midstream
processing is concentrated in Asia, which means the Andes are negotiating over
the least profitable step of the chain. Moving up it is the stated ambition of
every government in this group.

> The historical question in the Andes was who owned the mountain. The current
> one is who owns the refinery.

## Reading it on the map

Compare this group against the GDP per capita and extreme-poverty layers. The
boom years are visible in the aggregates. What the aggregates do not show is how
unevenly the mining districts themselves fared, which is what the politics of
the region has been about since Potosi.

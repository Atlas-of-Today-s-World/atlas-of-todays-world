Vladimir Putin took office as acting president on the last day of 1999, presented
as a technocratic stabiliser after a decade of economic collapse. A quarter of a
century later the office he holds bears little resemblance to the one he
inherited. What changed was not a single constitutional rupture but a long series
of ordinary-looking adjustments, each defensible on its own terms, which together
removed every institution capable of saying no.

## The three phases

**Consolidation (2000–2008).** The first term dismantled the independent
television channels and brought the regional governors under central appointment.
High oil prices paid for a genuine rise in living standards, which bought consent
for the political narrowing that accompanied it.

**Managed succession (2008–2012).** The constitutional two-term limit was
respected in form: Dmitry Medvedev held the presidency while Putin served as
prime minister. The arrangement demonstrated that the formal rules could be
satisfied without the distribution of power changing at all.

**Personalisation (2012–present).** The protests that followed the 2011
parliamentary elections marked the turn. Legislation on "foreign agents",
"undesirable organisations" and later on "discrediting the armed forces" moved
the boundaries of permitted speech steadily inward. The 2020 constitutional
amendments reset the presidential term count to zero.

## What holds it together

Three mechanisms do most of the work.

1. **Resource rent.** Hydrocarbon revenue funds the security services, the
   military and the public-sector wage bill without requiring the bargaining with
   taxpayers that a diversified economy would force.
2. **Elite dependency.** Property rights at the top are conditional. Wealth is
   held at the pleasure of the system, which makes defection costly in a way that
   ideology alone never could.
3. **Information management.** State television remains the primary news source
   for older voters, while the independent press has largely relocated abroad.

## Where it is fragile

The system's strength is also its constraint. Personalised rule solves the
problem of coordination among elites but leaves succession unresolved: there is
no mechanism for transferring authority that does not also transfer the ability
to prosecute predecessors. The war in Ukraine has deepened the dependence on the
security apparatus and narrowed the range of people whose advice reaches the
centre.

> Regimes of this type rarely fall because of opposition strength. They fall
> because the people inside them stop believing that the current arrangement is
> the safest one available.

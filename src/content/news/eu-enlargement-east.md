For most of the 2010s, enlargement was the European Union's dormant policy. The
full-scale invasion of Ukraine revived it within months, turning what had been a
technical process into the Union's central strategic question.

## Three candidates, three problems

**Ukraine** brings the strongest political case and the hardest practical one: a
large agricultural sector, wartime institutions, and territory under occupation.

**Moldova** is small enough to absorb but carries an unresolved separatist region
and deep energy dependence.

**Georgia** illustrates that candidate status is reversible in practice: the
governing party's legislative turn has effectively frozen the process.

## The internal arithmetic

Accession changes the maths of every existing policy. Cohesion funds and the
common agricultural policy would both need restructuring; qualified-majority
voting becomes unavoidable if unanimity is not to produce permanent deadlock.
Enlargement is therefore, for current members, primarily a debate about
themselves.

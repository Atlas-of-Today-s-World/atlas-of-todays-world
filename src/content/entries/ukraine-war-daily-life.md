The most striking thing about daily life in wartime Ukraine is how much of it
looks unremarkable. Cafés open, children take exams, trams run on schedule. The
war is present less as interruption than as a layer of logistics laid over
everything else.

## The grid decides the day

Strikes on generation and transmission capacity turned electricity into the
organising constraint of the working day. Households learned their district's
outage schedule the way they once learned tram timetables. Businesses that could
afford generators became informal public infrastructure: the café with power
becomes the neighbourhood office.

## Work reorganised

Remote work, already normalised by the pandemic, absorbed much of the shock. The
IT sector kept exporting throughout. Elsewhere the labour market has been
reshaped by mobilisation and by the departure of several million people, mostly
women and children, leaving acute shortages in healthcare, transport and
construction.

## What the numbers miss

Aggregate indicators capture the loss of output but not its distribution.
Frontline oblasts and the rest of the country now differ more from each other
than Ukraine as a whole differs from its neighbours.
